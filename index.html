Why am I being invited?

You are invited to take part in a student research project. Please read this page before deciding. Take as long as you need, and contact me if anything is unclear.

What is this project about?

A robot moving through a busy hospital corridor has to do two things at once, and they pull against each other. It must react instantly — if someone steps out, waiting even half a second is too late. But it should also understand the situation: that a group is standing talking rather than walking, or that someone is about to turn a corner. Understanding of that kind currently comes from large AI vision models, and those take one to three seconds to answer a single question about one camera image. The model used throughout this project is Gemma 4 26B-A4B, an open-weights vision-language model that runs on a single desktop graphics card — on our hardware it takes about three seconds per image.

So the robot cannot simply wait for the AI to tell it what to do. This project builds a robot control system split into two halves running at different speeds. A fast half re-plans about ten times a second and handles immediate obstacle avoidance. A slow half sends a camera image to an AI model and, a second or two later, gets back a short description of the social situation — how much room to leave, which side to pass, whether anything looks risky. The fast half treats that description as advice that gradually goes stale, trusting it less the older it gets, rather than as an instruction it must obey.

Everything is tested in simulation: a virtual hospital, a virtual robot, and simulated people who walk around and get out of the way. We can measure whether the robot reached its goal, how close it came to people, and how smoothly it moved. What we cannot measure is whether any of it looks right to a person, and that is the gap this survey fills.

What is the purpose of this survey?

Two specific questions that the simulation's own numbers cannot answer:

  1. Does the robot move in a way people find socially acceptable? A robot can score a perfect run — no collisions, goal reached — while still passing far too close, or stopping dead in a way that would feel alarming or rude in a real corridor.
  2. Is the AI model's reading of a scene actually correct? The model reports things like “someone is close on the left”. We want to know whether people looking at the same image agree, because if the model is confidently wrong, the robot is acting on bad advice.

What will I be asked?

Three short sections. There are no right or wrong answers — your impression is the data.

  • Section 1 — how the robot moved. You watch short animations, seen from above, of the robot travelling along a corridor while people walk past. For each one you rate how comfortable you would feel walking near that robot, whether it kept a sensible distance, and whether its movement looked natural or awkward.
  • Section 2 — what the robot's AI saw. You see the robot's own camera view alongside what the AI model reported about it. You say how well that reading matches what you see, how many people you can see, where the nearest person is, and how cautious you think the robot should be there.
  • Section 3 — comparing two robots. Side-by-side journeys along the same route, one guided by the AI model and one by a system given perfect information. You say which you would rather share a corridor with, and why.

A few optional questions at the end ask how familiar you are with robots, so we can check whether background changes how people answer. You may skip any question.

Do I have to take part?

No. Participation is entirely voluntary. If you decide not to take part, nothing happens and no one will know — the survey is anonymous, so I cannot tell who has responded. You need not give a reason, and it will not affect our relationship in any way.

What will happen if I take part?

One online questionnaire, once, taking about 15–20 minutes. You will see computer-generated images from a robotics simulation: overhead diagrams of paths taken by a simulated robot near simulated people, and simulated camera views with an AI model's description of them. You will give your own judgement using rating scales and multiple choice. There are no right or wrong answers, and nothing is being tested about you. No real people, places or robots appear in any image.

Are there risks?

No risks beyond those of everyday life are anticipated. The images show simple diagrams and simulated indoor scenes and contain no distressing content. You may skip any question and may close the browser at any point.

What data is collected, and is it anonymous?

Only your answers. No name, email address, IP address or anything else that could identify you is collected. Because responses are anonymous, it will not be possible to withdraw your data once you have submitted it, as I will have no way of identifying which response is yours. You may withdraw at any point before submitting by closing the browser.

How will my data be used?

Anonymous responses will be stored on King's College London approved storage, analysed as part of my final-year project report, and may appear in aggregate form (for example, average ratings) in that report and any resulting publication. Individual responses will never be reported in a way that could identify anyone.

Section 2 — what the robot's AI saw

The robot's own camera views, with what the AI model reported about each. Tell us whether you agree with its reading.

What the AI model reports

The model is Gemma 4 26B-A4B. For every camera image it fills in the same eight items. Two are distances and speeds in ordinary units; the rest are ratings, shown here out of 10.

itemwhat it means
behaviourThe overall stance to take: normal (carry on), cautious (slow down and keep clear), or social bypass (deliberately go around someone rather than through).
space to leaveHow much clear space the robot should try to keep between itself and the nearest person, in metres. For scale, an arm's length is about 0.7 m.
speed limitThe fastest the robot should travel here, in metres per second. A relaxed walking pace is about 1.4 m/s, so 0.7 m/s is roughly half walking speed.
where the risk isWhich part of the view needs watching — ahead, to the front-left, to the front-right, or nowhere in particular.
which side to passWhether to favour going left, going right, or has no preference.
caution needed0 = nothing here to worry about, 10 = treat this as a hazard.
attention to peopleHow much the people in view should influence the robot's choices. 0 = they are irrelevant here, 10 = they should drive the decision.
model's confidenceHow sure the model is of its own answer. 0 = a guess, 10 = certain. A confident wrong answer is worth telling us about.
